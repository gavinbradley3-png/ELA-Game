# Receipts: Evidence Battle

## 1. Product Name and Core Concept

### Product Name

**Receipts**

### MVP Mode

**Evidence Battle**

### One-Sentence Concept

**Receipts is a teacher-led live ELA game where students compete to make the most defensible claim by selecting strong textual evidence, explaining their reasoning, evaluating anonymous peer responses, revising their work, and discussing class-wide thinking.**

### Elevator Pitch

Receipts turns a live reading lesson into a structured literacy battle. Students read a shared passage, annotate with purpose, choose evidence, make a claim, explain how their evidence proves their thinking, compare anonymous peer responses, revise their own answer, and reflect on what changed. The teacher controls every phase, sees class-wide reasoning patterns, spotlights strong thinking, exposes weak evidence habits, and leads discussion from real student work.

### Plain-English Explanation

Students do not just answer a question. They must bring the **receipt**: a piece of text that proves their interpretation. Then they must explain why that evidence matters. Other students anonymously compare responses and decide which one is stronger using a rubric. Students revise after feedback. The teacher uses the results to drive discussion.

### Classroom Problem It Solves

Traditional reading questions often produce shallow answers, random quotes, or one-sentence opinions. Whole-class discussion usually depends on the same few students. Receipts forces every student to engage with the text, justify their thinking, evaluate others, and revise. It gives the teacher a fast view of what the whole class understands, misunderstands, and needs next.

### What Students Actually Do During a Round

Students:

1. Join a live round with a code.
2. Read a teacher-selected passage.
3. Annotate targeted parts of the text.
4. Respond to a challenge prompt.
5. Select evidence from the passage.
6. Write a claim.
7. Explain how the evidence supports the claim.
8. Compare two anonymous peer responses.
9. Justify which response is stronger.
10. Revise their original response.
11. Reflect on what improved.

### What Teachers Get Out of It

Teachers get:

- A structured 45–50 minute live ELA lesson.
- More students participating.
- Evidence of student comprehension and reasoning.
- Class-wide trends in evidence selection.
- Examples worth discussing.
- Weak reasoning patterns to address immediately.
- Revision data showing whether students improved.
- Exportable formative assessment results.

### Why This Is Better Than a Quiz Game or Worksheet

A quiz game rewards speed and recall. A worksheet often rewards completion. Receipts rewards **thinking quality**.

Students cannot win by clicking quickly, guessing, or writing more words. They must interpret, prove, explain, compare, revise, and reflect. The competition creates urgency, but the academic purpose stays dominant: better evidence-based reasoning.

## 2. Product Vision

### Long-Term Vision

Receipts should become the fastest way for an ELA teacher to turn any short text into a live, discussion-rich literacy lesson where every student must read, annotate, reason, revise, and participate.

It should support repeated classroom use across Grades 7–9 with short stories, articles, poems, novel excerpts, speeches, persuasive texts, informational texts, and media transcripts.

### What It Should Become

Receipts should become:

- A live classroom literacy game.
- A formative assessment tool.
- A reasoning practice system.
- A teacher discussion engine.
- A passage-and-prompt workspace.
- A safe structure for peer evaluation.
- A way to make revision normal, visible, and meaningful.

### What It Should Never Become

Receipts must never become:

- A homework platform.
- A self-paced reading website.
- A Kahoot clone.
- A worksheet generator.
- A full LMS.
- A gradebook replacement.
- A bloated standards-reporting system.
- A fake gamified economy with coins, stores, pets, or cosmetics.
- A social platform.
- A place where students freely wander without teacher direction.

### Learning Philosophy

Students improve when they repeatedly practice the full reasoning chain:

> **Text → annotation → evidence → claim → explanation → peer evaluation → revision → reflection**

The product must make that chain unavoidable.

### Why Live Classroom Play Matters

Live play matters because the teacher can:

- Read the room.
- Adjust pacing.
- Pause for discussion.
- Catch confusion immediately.
- Spotlight student thinking.
- Protect struggling students.
- Keep students accountable.
- Turn results into conversation while the text is still fresh.

Asynchronous work loses that energy and control.

### Why Teacher Control Matters

Junior high students need structure. If students can skip ahead, wander, vote randomly, or submit junk to unlock the next step, the product fails. The teacher must control the round because the product is a live lesson, not an independent activity.

### Why Revision Is Central

Revision is the learning move. First answers show current thinking. Revised answers show growth. The product is weak if students only submit once and move on. The MVP must protect time for revision.

### Why Evidence-Based Reasoning Is the Core Skill

ELA growth depends on students learning to prove interpretations with text. Opinion without evidence is not analysis. Evidence without reasoning is quote-dropping. Receipts must train students to connect both.

### North Star Statement

**Receipts helps every student prove their thinking with stronger evidence and clearer reasoning during one live class period.**

## 3. Problem Statement

### The Real Classroom Problem

Students often complete reading tasks without doing real interpretation. They answer quickly, choose random evidence, over-highlight, or write vague opinions. Teachers then have to infer what students actually understood from messy written answers or limited class discussion.

### Specific Problems

| Problem | Classroom Reality | Product Response |
|---|---|---|
| Students choose weak or random evidence | Students grab the first quote that sounds related. | Require evidence selection from the passage and make students explain why it proves the claim. |
| Students struggle to explain why evidence matters | Students write “This proves it because it says so.” | Require a reasoning field with prompts that push cause, inference, connection, and interpretation. |
| Students confuse opinion with interpretation | Students write personal reactions instead of text-based claims. | Challenge prompts must require defensible interpretations supported by evidence. |
| Students over-highlight without purpose | Students highlight whole paragraphs or everything that seems important. | Use annotation tags, highlight limits, and teacher heatmap visibility. |
| Students give shallow peer feedback | Students write “good” or vote randomly. | Use anonymous side-by-side comparison with required justification. |
| Teachers lack fast ways to see reasoning gaps | Teachers cannot scan 30 responses instantly during class. | Dashboard clusters evidence choices, common claims, weak reasoning, and revision changes. |
| Traditional questions do not always create discussion | Questions become silent written work. | Reveal trends and anonymous examples to launch discussion. |
| Whole-class discussion relies on few students | Confident students dominate. | Every student submits, reviews, revises, and reflects before discussion. |

### How the Game Solves the Problem

Receipts creates a live structure where all students must:

1. Read the same text.
2. Annotate for a purpose.
3. Make a claim.
4. Select evidence.
5. Explain reasoning.
6. Evaluate peer reasoning.
7. Revise based on feedback.
8. Reflect on improvement.

The teacher gets immediate instructional insight and can lead discussion from actual student thinking instead of guessing where the class is.

## 7. Primary MVP Game Mode: Evidence Battle

## Purpose

Evidence Battle trains students to make a defensible interpretation by selecting the strongest textual evidence and explaining how that evidence supports a claim.

This is the correct MVP because it targets the highest-value ELA skill: **using evidence to support reasoning**.

## Core Gameplay

Students receive a passage and challenge prompt. They annotate the passage, select the best evidence, write a claim, explain their reasoning, submit, compare anonymous peer responses, justify which is stronger, revise their own response, then view class trends and reflect.

## Round Setup

Teacher selects or creates:

- Class
- Passage
- Challenge prompt
- Focus skill
- Allowed annotation tags
- Timers
- Peer review anonymity setting
- Partner mode setting, if used
- Reveal settings

## Student Task

Students must submit:

1. Selected evidence quote or passage range.
2. Annotation tag.
3. Claim.
4. Reasoning explanation.
5. Confidence rating.
6. Peer comparison decision.
7. Peer justification.
8. Revised response.
9. Reflection.

## Teacher Controls

Teacher controls:

- Phase progression.
- Timers.
- Submission locks.
- Peer review start.
- Revision start.
- Name visibility.
- Spotlighting.
- Response removal.
- Reveal timing.
- Export.

## Submission Requirements

A complete first submission requires:

| Field | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Evidence | Selected from passage; must not exceed configured quote length unless teacher allows. |
| Annotation tag | Required; must match selected evidence purpose. |
| Claim | Required; recommended 20–180 characters. |
| Reasoning | Required; recommended 80–500 characters. |
| Confidence | Required; 1–4 scale. |
| Counterargument | Optional; recommended for advanced students. |

## Peer Comparison Rules

- Students see two anonymous responses.
- Students cannot see names.
- Students cannot review their own response.
- Responses are randomized.
- The system balances how often each response is seen.
- Students choose which response is stronger based on a visible rubric.
- Students must justify their choice.
- Teacher can remove a response from the pool.

## Revision Rules

Students can revise:

- Evidence selection.
- Annotation tag.
- Claim.
- Reasoning.
- Optional counterargument.

Students must answer:

> What did you change, and why is it stronger now?

A revision is meaningful only if it improves precision, relevance, reasoning, accuracy, or clarity. Adding filler does not count.

## Scoring Logic

### MVP Scoring

The MVP should use a simple formative score, not a complex algorithm.

| Category | Points | Notes |
|---|---:|---|
| Purposeful annotation | 0–2 | Relevant tag and reasonable highlight range. |
| Evidence quality | 0–4 | Precise, relevant, supports prompt. |
| Claim quality | 0–4 | Specific, defensible, answers prompt. |
| Reasoning quality | 0–4 | Explains how evidence proves claim. |
| Peer review quality | 0–3 | Thoughtful comparison and justification. |
| Revision quality | 0–4 | Meaningful improvement from first draft. |
| Reflection | 0–2 | Names specific learning or change. |

**Total: 23 points**

This score should support formative feedback. It should not become the central game experience.

## Reveal Format

Reveal should show:

- Most selected evidence ranges.
- Strong evidence clusters.
- Weak evidence clusters.
- Common claims.
- Common reasoning gaps.
- Anonymous strong responses.
- Before/after revision examples.
- Discussion prompts.

Reveal should not show:

- Public ranking of all students.
- Weak student names.
- Fastest submitters.
- Popularity as correctness.
- Embarrassing failure data.

## Teacher Dashboard Output

Dashboard must show:

- Joined students.
- Submission status.
- Evidence heatmap.
- Response quality flags.
- Peer review patterns.
- Revision improvement.
- Strong examples.
- Misconceptions.
- Suggested discussion prompts.
- Exportable round summary.

## Class Discussion Use

The teacher can ask:

- “Which evidence was strongest, and why?”
- “Which popular evidence choice was actually weak?”
- “What changed between the first draft and revision?”
- “What makes this reasoning better than just repeating the quote?”
- “What would make this claim more defensible?”

## Why This Mode Is the Correct MVP

Evidence Battle is narrow enough to build well and important enough to matter. It targets reading, annotation, evidence, claim-making, reasoning, peer evaluation, revision, and discussion in one repeatable structure.

Building multiple modes first would be sloppy. One excellent mode beats five half-built modes.

## 9. Round Structure for a 45–50 Minute Class

## Standard 45–50 Minute Round

| Time | Phase | Activity | Notes |
|---:|---|---|---|
| Before class | Teacher setup | Select passage, prompt, tags, timers. | Target setup time: 3–7 minutes. |
| 0–3 min | Join / Lobby | Students enter code and display name. | Teacher removes bad names immediately. |
| 3–9 min | Reading | Students read passage silently or with teacher read-aloud. | Keep passage short enough for one class. |
| 9–15 min | Annotation | Students highlight and tag purposeful evidence. | Limit highlights. Focus on prompt-related reading. |
| 15–17 min | Challenge Prompt | Teacher reveals prompt and clarifies task. | Do not over-explain the answer. |
| 17–25 min | Submission | Students select evidence, write claim, explain reasoning. | Teacher monitors stuck students. |
| 25–31 min | Peer Comparison | Students compare anonymous responses and justify choice. | No names. No friend voting. |
| 31–38 min | Revision | Students revise and explain what changed. | This is central. Protect it. |
| 38–43 min | Reveal | Show evidence clusters, strong responses, common mistakes. | Teacher controls reveal. |
| 43–48 min | Teacher-Led Discussion | Discuss evidence, reasoning, revision, misconceptions. | Use anonymous student work. |
| 48–50 min | Exit Reflection | Students answer one short reflection prompt. | Keep it brief and specific. |

### What Gets Protected

- Reading
- Evidence selection
- Reasoning
- Peer comparison
- Revision
- Discussion

### What Can Be Shortened

- Lobby
- Annotation time
- Number of peer comparisons
- Reveal depth
- Reflection length

### What Should Never Be Skipped

Revision should not be skipped unless the class is out of time or technology fails. If forced to cut something, cut scoring, badges, or extended reveal. Do not cut the learning move.

## Short 25–30 Minute Round

| Time | Phase | Activity | What Changes |
|---:|---|---|---|
| Before class | Teacher setup | Use saved passage and prompt. | No custom setup during class. |
| 0–2 min | Join | Students join fast. | Lock late join after reading starts if needed. |
| 2–7 min | Reading | Short passage only. | Use poem, excerpt, paragraph, short article section. |
| 7–10 min | Annotation | Students select 1–2 key annotations. | Hard highlight limit. |
| 10–16 min | Submission | Evidence, claim, reasoning. | Counterargument disabled. |
| 16–20 min | Peer Comparison | One comparison only. | Required justification stays. |
| 20–25 min | Revision | Students revise one part. | Revision stays, but is focused. |
| 25–28 min | Reveal | Show one evidence trend and one strong example. | No full dashboard discussion. |
| 28–30 min | Reflection | One sentence: “My reasoning improved because…” | Exit ticket style. |

### What Gets Cut

- Multiple peer comparisons.
- Extended annotation.
- Deep dashboard reveal.
- Full class discussion.
- Optional counterargument.
- Badges.

### What Stays

- Reading.
- Evidence.
- Claim.
- Reasoning.
- Peer comparison.
- Revision.
- Reflection.

### What Should Never Be Skipped

Do not skip evidence selection, reasoning, or revision. Without those, the round collapses into a shallow quiz or worksheet.

## 10. Teacher Setup Flow

## Setup Goal

A teacher should be able to create a usable round in **3–7 minutes** if they already have a passage, and under **2 minutes** if using a saved passage and prompt.

## Setup Flow

1. Create or select class.
2. Create or select passage.
3. Add passage metadata.
4. Create or select challenge prompt.
5. Select focus skill.
6. Choose allowed annotation tags.
7. Set timers.
8. Choose peer review settings.
9. Preview student view.
10. Launch round.

## Setup Fields

| Field | Required? | Smart Default | Can Skip? | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Class | Required | Most recent class | No | Needed for teacher organization. |
| Passage title | Required | Untitled Passage | No | Must identify round. |

## 15. Annotation System

## Annotation Mechanics

Students select text and apply a tag. Tags must be limited per round. The teacher chooses allowed tags to match the focus skill.

MVP should include only a small tag set by default:

- Important evidence
- Inference
- Character
- Theme
- Conflict
- Question
- Confusion

Additional tags can exist but should be teacher-enabled.

## Annotation Tags

| Tag | Definition | When Students Should Use It | Strong Example | Weak Example | Teacher Tip | MVP? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Important evidence | Text that helps prove a claim. | When a quote directly supports the prompt. | Highlighting a line where a character admits fear for a motivation prompt. | Highlighting a random dramatic sentence. | Ask: “What does this prove?” | Yes |
| Character | Text revealing traits, choices, relationships, or motivation. | When analyzing a character. | Highlighting repeated avoidance that shows guilt. | Highlighting the character’s name only. | Push students from action to motive. | Yes |
| Theme | Text revealing a larger message. | When evidence connects to a lesson or idea. | Highlighting a moment where selfishness causes harm. | Highlighting a random quote about sadness. | Theme evidence usually connects beyond one event. | Yes |
| Conflict | Text showing struggle or tension. | Internal, external, social, moral, or emotional conflict. | Highlighting a character choosing between loyalty and honesty. | Highlighting any argument without explaining tension. | Ask students to name the opposing forces. | Yes |
| Tone | Text showing author’s attitude. | When diction reveals attitude. | Highlighting sarcastic word choice in narration. | Highlighting any emotional sentence. | Ask: “What attitude does this create?” | Later |
| Mood | Text creating atmosphere or feeling. | When setting, imagery, or events shape reader feeling. | Highlighting storm imagery that creates dread. | Highlighting “it was dark” without explanation. | Mood is reader feeling, not author attitude. | Later |
| Symbolism | Object/image/action with deeper meaning. | When something represents more than itself. | Highlighting a broken medal that represents lost pride. | Highlighting any object. | Require students to explain what it represents. | Later |
| Setting | Time/place/social context affecting meaning. | When place or time shapes conflict or mood. | Highlighting a crowded hallway where public shame matters. | Highlighting a location name only. | Ask how setting changes the stakes. | Later |
| Prediction | A clue about what may happen next. | During early reading or suspense. | Highlighting repeated warnings before a disaster. | Guessing without text clue. | Prediction must be evidence-based. | Later |
| Question | A real question about meaning. | When confused or curious about interpretation. | “Why does the narrator avoid naming the friend?” | “What does this word mean?” if vocabulary only. | Questions can fuel discussion. | Yes |
| Confusion | Text that is unclear. | When students need support. | Highlighting a confusing shift in speaker. | Highlighting everything because reading is hard. | Use this to identify support needs. | Yes |
| Inference | Text that suggests something indirectly. | When meaning is implied. | Highlighting a trembling hand to infer fear. | Highlighting a direct statement like “I am scared.” | Ask: “What does the text suggest?” | Yes |
| Author’s craft | Writer’s technique that creates meaning. | Imagery, repetition, contrast, structure, diction. | Highlighting repeated silence to show tension. | Highlighting a cool phrase without effect. | Require effect on reader or meaning. | Later |
| Persuasive technique | Method used to influence audience. | Appeals, repetition, loaded language, evidence. | Highlighting emotional appeal in a speech. | Highlighting a fact without persuasive function. | Ask what the technique makes the audience think or feel. | Later |
| Bias | One-sided, loaded, or unfair framing. | When language reveals partiality. | Highlighting “reckless outsiders” in an article. | Highlighting an opinion you dislike. | Bias must be shown through wording or omission. | Later |
| Counterargument | Opposing view or complication. | When text presents another side. | Highlighting a paragraph acknowledging critics. | Highlighting unrelated disagreement. | Useful for advanced argument work. | Later |

## Safeguards Against Over-Highlighting

| Safeguard | How It Works |
|---|---|
| Highlight limits | Teacher sets maximum highlights or total highlighted words. |
| Prompted annotation purpose | Students see the focus skill and allowed tags. |
| Required explanation for selected evidence | Highlighting alone does not earn credit. |
| Visual warning | “You have highlighted a lot. Choose only what helps prove the prompt.” |
| Teacher heatmap | Teacher sees students who highlighted excessive text. |
| Quote length limit | Evidence selection cannot be an entire passage. |
| Tag restriction | Teacher limits tags to the day’s skill. |

## 16. Challenge Prompt System

## Prompt Types

| Prompt Type | Purpose | Strong Prompt | Weak Prompt | Why Weak Prompt Is Weak | Thinking It Should Produce |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Theme | Identify message supported by evidence. | “What message does the text suggest about loyalty, and which moment best proves it?” | “What is the theme?” | Too broad and invites guessing. | Thematic claim tied to a key moment. |
| Character motivation | Infer why a character acts. | “Why does Maya refuse help at the end of the scene? Use evidence to support your interpretation.” | “What did Maya do?” | Recall only. | Motivation inference from action/dialogue. |
| Conflict | Analyze struggle. | “Which conflict matters most in this excerpt, and how does it shape the character’s choice?” | “Is there conflict?” | Yes/no and shallow. | Identification and significance of conflict. |
| Tone | Interpret author attitude. | “How does the narrator’s tone toward the town change, and which words reveal the shift?” | “What words are interesting?” | No interpretive target. | Diction-based tone analysis. |
| Mood | Analyze atmosphere. | “How does the author create a feeling of unease in this scene?” | “How did you feel?” | Personal reaction only. | Evidence about imagery, setting, pacing. |
| Inference | Draw supported conclusion. | “What can we infer about Jonah’s relationship with his father, even though it is not directly stated?” | “Who is Jonah’s father?” | Recall or fact lookup. | Inference from indirect clues. |
| Author’s purpose | Explain why the author included something. | “Why does the author begin with the argument before revealing the setting?” | “What happens first?” | Sequence only. | Structure and effect analysis. |
| Argument analysis | Evaluate persuasive strength. | “Which piece of evidence best supports the speaker’s argument, and why is it convincing?” | “Do you agree?” | Opinion without textual reasoning. | Evidence evaluation and persuasion analysis. |
| Symbolism | Interpret deeper meaning. | “What does the cracked photograph symbolize, and which detail best supports that interpretation?” | “Find a symbol.” | Too open without reasoning requirement. | Symbol-to-theme connection. |
| Compare and contrast | Analyze meaningful similarity/difference. | “How do the two characters respond differently to pressure, and what evidence proves the contrast?” | “How are they alike and different?” | Too broad and list-like. | Focused comparison with evidence. |
| Best evidence | Judge strongest support. | “Which line best proves that the narrator is unreliable?” | “Find a quote.” | No quality judgment. | Evidence ranking and explanation. |
| Most defensible claim | Choose/support strongest interpretation. | “Which claim about the ending is most defensible based on the text?” | “What did you think of the ending?” | Personal preference. | Claim evaluation based on evidence. |
| Misleading evidence | Recognize evidence that appears useful but fails. | “Which evidence seems related but does not actually prove the claim?” | “Which quote is bad?” | Vague and negative. | Precision about relevance and proof. |
| Weak vs strong reasoning | Evaluate explanation quality. | “Which explanation better connects the quote to the claim, and why?” | “Which answer is better?” | No criteria. | Reasoning comparison. |
| Counterargument | Consider competing interpretations. | “What is the strongest counterargument to this claim, and how could the text respond?” | “What else could someone say?” | Too vague. | Balanced interpretation. |
| Author’s craft | Analyze technique and effect. | “How does repetition in the final paragraph affect the meaning of the scene?” | “Find repetition.” | Identification without analysis. | Technique, evidence, effect. |

## Prompt Quality Rules

Strong prompts must:

- Require interpretation.
- Require evidence.
- Allow more than one defensible answer.
- Be specific enough to focus thinking.
- Avoid simple recall.
- Avoid yes/no answers.
- Avoid vague “What do you think?” questions.
- Connect to one primary skill.
- Be answerable within the selected passage.
- Produce discussion-worthy disagreement.

Weak prompts:

- Ask for recall only.
- Ask for personal opinion only.
- Are too broad.
- Are too vague.
- Have only one obvious answer.
- Do not require evidence.
- Cannot be answered from the passage.
- Encourage guessing.

## 18. Peer Review and Voting System

## Core Principle

Peer review is not popularity voting. It is anonymous academic comparison.

## Peer Review Flow

1. Student submits first response.
2. Teacher triggers peer review.
3. System assigns two anonymous responses.
4. Student reads both.
5. Student uses visible rubric criteria.
6. Student chooses the stronger response.
7. Student justifies the choice.
8. System saves vote and justification.
9. Teacher sees patterns.
10. Student uses feedback to revise.

## Criteria Students Use

Students evaluate:

- Evidence quality.
- Reasoning quality.
- Connection to prompt.
- Accuracy.
- Depth.
- Clarity.
- Specificity.
- Usefulness of explanation.

## Scoring Logic

Peer review score should reward:

| Behaviour | Reward |
|---|---|
| Chooses stronger response with clear justification | High peer review score |
| Uses rubric language | High peer review score |
| Explains evidence/reasoning difference | High peer review score |
| Gives vague justification | Low score and flag |
| Clicks without meaningful reason | Low score and flag |
| Repeats same justification | Flag for teacher |
| Always picks left/right | Pattern flag |

## Anti-Friend-Voting Safeguards

- Anonymous responses.
- No names during review.
- Randomized pairings.
- Students cannot review themselves.
- Response order randomized.
- Teacher can hide all identifying details.
- Peer review results are not treated as perfect truth.
- Teacher can override or remove responses.
- Low-effort justifications flagged.
- Repeated identical justifications flagged.

## What Teacher Sees

Teacher sees:

- Which responses were frequently selected.
- Which responses had strong peer justifications.
- Which students gave low-effort peer feedback.
- Which criteria students used.
- Disagreement patterns.
- Responses worth discussing.

## What Students See

Students see:

- Two anonymous responses.
- Rubric criteria.
- Their selected choice.
- Their own review task.
- Later, limited feedback to support revision.

Students do not see:

- Names.
- Full class rankings.
- Who voted for them.
- Public popularity leaderboard.

## How Peer Review Feeds Revision

Before revising, students see:

- Their original response.
- A reminder of rubric criteria.
- A summary of peer comparison feedback if available.
- Teacher-selected model examples if teacher reveals them.
- Prompt: “What will you improve: evidence, claim, reasoning, or clarity?”

## 19. Revision System

## Core Principle

Revision is not cleanup. Revision is the proof that learning happened.

## What Students See Before Revising

Students see:

- Original evidence.
- Original claim.
- Original reasoning.
- Peer comparison feedback, if available.
- Rubric reminders.
- Teacher spotlight examples, if released.
- Specific revision prompt.

## Feedback Students Receive

Students may receive:

- “Your evidence was selected less often than alternatives.”
- “Your reasoning may need a clearer connection to the claim.”
- “Peer reviewers preferred responses that explained why the quote mattered.”
- “Your evidence is relevant, but broad.”
- “Your claim is defensible, but needs precision.”

## What Students Can Edit

Students can edit:

- Evidence selection.
- Annotation tag.
- Claim.
- Reasoning.
- Counterargument.

## What Students Must Explain After Revising

Students must answer:

> What did you change, and why is it stronger now?

Required revision explanation should identify at least one category:

- Evidence.
- Claim.
- Reasoning.
- Accuracy.
- Specificity.
- Clarity.

## Revision Scoring

Revision should be scored on improvement, not length.

| Revision Quality | Description |
|---|---|
| No meaningful revision | Same answer or filler added. |
| Surface revision | Fixes wording but not thinking. |
| Meaningful revision | Improves evidence, claim, or reasoning. |
| Strong revision | Clearly strengthens the logic between evidence and claim. |

## How Teacher Sees Improvement

Teacher dashboard compares:

- First evidence vs revised evidence.
- First claim vs revised claim.
- First reasoning vs revised reasoning.
- Student explanation of change.
- Rubric movement.

## Weak Revision Example

Original:

> “The character is nervous. The quote is ‘he looked down.’ This proves he is nervous because he looked down.”

Revision:

> “The character is really nervous. The quote is ‘he looked down.’ This proves he is very nervous because he looked down.”

Problem:

- Adds intensity but not reasoning.
- Does not explain why looking down shows nervousness.
- No stronger evidence.

## Strong Revision Example

Original:

> “The character is nervous because he looked down.”

Revision:

> “The character feels guilty, not just nervous, because he avoids eye contact right after being asked about the missing money. Looking down matters because it happens at the exact moment he has a reason to hide the truth.”

Why stronger:

- More precise claim.
- Contextualizes evidence.
- Explains how action supports inference.

## 20. Scoring and Progression

## Scoring Principles

Scoring should reward learning behaviours, not game loopholes.

Reward:

- Purposeful annotation.
- Strong evidence.
- Clear claim.
- Clear reasoning.
- Accurate connection to prompt.
- Thoughtful peer review.
- Meaningful revision.
- Reflection.
- Improvement over time.

Do not over-reward:

- Speed.
- Random highlighting.
- Popularity.
- Length.
- Clicking everything.
- Friend votes.
- First draft only.

## MVP Scoring Model

Use a simple formative score.

| Category | Points |
|---|---:|
| Annotation quality | 0–2 |
| Evidence quality | 0–4 |
| Claim strength | 0–4 |
| Reasoning quality | 0–4 |
| Peer review justification | 0–3 |
| Revision quality | 0–4 |
| Reflection | 0–2 |
| Total | 23 |

## Future Scoring Model

Later versions may include:

- Growth over multiple rounds.
- Skill-specific trends.
- Teacher-adjusted rubric weighting.
- Badge-based recognition.
- Class goal progress.

Do not build complex scoring before teachers confirm the MVP data is useful.

## Individual Scoring

Individual scores should be private by default. Students can see their own progress but not a public rank list.

## Class / Team Scoring

Class scoring can be useful if it rewards group literacy behaviour.

Acceptable class goals:

- “80% of the class used specific evidence.”
- “Class reasoning improved after revision.”
- “Most peer reviews included rubric language.”

Avoid toxic scoring:

- Public bottom rankings.
- Fastest student wins.
- Popularity leaderboard.
- Weakest response reveal.

## Avoid Embarrassing Struggling Students

- Hide names by default.
- Spotlight strong work anonymously.
- Show class trends, not individual failures.
- Let teacher privately identify support needs.
- Recognize improvement, not just top performance.

## Badges

Badges are optional and should wait unless they reinforce learning.

| Badge | Behaviour That Earns It | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| Evidence Sniper | Selects precise evidence that directly proves the claim. | Reinforces precision. |
| Revision Master | Makes a meaningful improvement after feedback. | Makes revision valuable. |
| Inference Builder | Explains an implied meaning using evidence. | Supports deeper reading. |
| Theme Tracker | Connects evidence to a defensible theme. | Builds literary interpretation. |
| Reasoning Upgrade | Improves explanation from first draft to revision. | Rewards growth. |
| Annotation Architect | Uses purposeful tags without over-highlighting. | Builds annotation discipline. |
| Counterargument Catcher | Identifies and responds to another defensible view. | Builds advanced reasoning. |
| Precision Highlighter | Highlights only the words needed. | Fights over-highlighting. |

## 21. Rubrics and Assessment

## 4-Level Rubric

### Annotation Quality

| Level | Description |
|---|---|
| 1. Beginning | Highlights are random, excessive, missing, or unrelated to the prompt. |
| 2. Developing | Highlights are somewhat related but broad, overused, or weakly tagged. |
| 3. Proficient | Highlights are relevant, purposeful, and tagged appropriately. |
| 4. Advanced | Highlights are precise, focused, and show insight into the prompt or skill. |

### Evidence Selection

| Level | Description |
|---|---|
| 1. Beginning | Evidence is missing, irrelevant, too broad, or does not support the claim. |
| 2. Developing | Evidence is related to the topic but only partly supports the claim. |
| 3. Proficient | Evidence clearly supports the claim and fits the prompt. |
| 4. Advanced | Evidence is precise, powerful, and reveals deeper meaning or complexity. |

### Claim Strength

| Level | Description |
|---|---|
| 1. Beginning | Claim is missing, unclear, one-word, or mostly opinion. |
| 2. Developing | Claim answers the prompt but is vague or too obvious. |
| 3. Proficient | Claim is specific, defensible, and text-based. |
| 4. Advanced | Claim is precise, insightful, and allows for meaningful discussion. |

### Reasoning

| Level | Description |
|---|---|
| 1. Beginning | Reasoning is missing, repeats the quote, or does not connect to the claim. |
| 2. Developing | Reasoning explains somewhat but leaves the connection unclear. |
| 3. Proficient | Reasoning clearly explains how the evidence supports the claim. |
| 4. Advanced | Reasoning explains significance, inference, and deeper connection to the prompt. |

### Peer Feedback

| Level | Description |
|---|---|
| 1. Beginning | Vote is random or justification is missing/useless. |
| 2. Developing | Justification names a preference but gives limited explanation. |
| 3. Proficient | Justification uses criteria such as evidence, reasoning, or clarity. |
| 4. Advanced | Justification compares both responses specifically and accurately. |

### Revision

| Level | Description |
|---|---|
| 1. Beginning | No meaningful change or only filler added. |
| 2. Developing | Some wording improved but evidence/reasoning remains weak. |
| 3. Proficient | Revision improves evidence, claim, or reasoning. |
| 4. Advanced | Revision clearly strengthens the logic and shows thoughtful improvement. |

### Reflection

| Level | Description |
|---|---|
| 1. Beginning | Reflection is vague, missing, or unrelated. |
| 2. Developing | Reflection names a general improvement. |
| 3. Proficient | Reflection identifies a specific change and why it helped. |
| 4. Advanced | Reflection explains growth and names a next step. |

## Quick Formative Assessment View

Teacher should see:

- Red/yellow/green indicators by category.
- Students missing required parts.
- Strong responses.
- Revision improvement.
- Common class need.

## Exportable Teacher Summary

Export should include:

- Round title.
- Passage title.
- Prompt.
- Student first and revised submissions.
- Rubric indicators.
- Peer review summary.
- Reflection responses.
- Teacher notes.

## Avoid Turning the Game Into Tedious Grading

The dashboard should help teachers make quick formative judgments. It should not force teachers to grade every sentence during class.

## What Should Be Auto-Scored

Auto-score or auto-flag:

- Missing fields.
- Character minimums.
- Highlight amount.
- Quote length.
- Submission completion.
- Peer justification length.
- Unchanged revision.
- Repeated peer justification.
- Inappropriate language flags.

## What Requires Teacher Judgment

Teacher judgment is required for:

- Evidence quality.
- Claim defensibility.
- Reasoning strength.
- Interpretation accuracy.
- Quality of revision.
- Whether a response is worth spotlighting.
- Whether a misconception needs instruction.

## 22. Feedback System

## Feedback Principles

Feedback must improve thinking. Generic praise is useless.

Avoid:

- “Good job.”
- “Try harder.”
- “Add more detail.”
- “Incorrect.”
- “Nice work.”

Use specific, actionable feedback.

## Feedback Types

| Feedback Type | When It Appears | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Immediate feedback | During submission. | Prevent missing or low-effort work. |
| Peer feedback | After comparison. | Show how others judged response quality. |
| Teacher feedback | During or after round. | Provide expert correction. |
| System hints | While writing. | Prompt better reasoning. |
| Revision prompts | Before revision. | Focus improvement. |
| Reflection questions | Exit phase. | Consolidate learning. |

## Useful Feedback Examples

| Weak Area | Useful Feedback |
|---|---|
| Evidence relevance | “Your evidence matches the topic, but it does not directly prove your claim.” |
| Motivation inference | “This quote shows what the character did, but not why they did it.” |
| Broad evidence | “Your claim is defensible, but your evidence is too broad. Choose the exact line that proves it.” |
| Quote repeat | “Your explanation repeats the quote. Explain what the reader should infer from it.” |
| Weak claim | “Your claim is too general. Make a specific interpretation that someone could debate.” |
| Revision improvement | “Your revision improved because you connected the quote directly to the prompt.” |
| Peer review | “A strong peer review compares evidence and reasoning, not which answer sounds nicer.” |

## Feedback Templates

### Evidence Feedback

> Your evidence is related to the prompt, but it needs to prove the claim more directly. Choose a smaller, more precise quote.

### Claim Feedback

> Your claim needs to be more specific. Make an interpretation that can be proven with the text.

### Reasoning Feedback

> Your reasoning needs to explain how the evidence proves the claim. Do not just restate the quote.

### Peer Review Feedback

> Your peer review must explain why one response is stronger using evidence, reasoning, clarity, or accuracy.

### Revision Feedback

> Your revision should improve the quality of your thinking, not just add more words. Identify what changed and why it is stronger.

### Reflection Feedback

> Name one specific change you made and explain how it improved your response.

## 23. Classroom Management and Safety Features

## Required Features

- Join code.
- Lock round.
- Pause round.
- Advance phase manually.
- Remove inappropriate response.
- Spotlight response.
- Hide names.
- Reset student submission.
- Timer controls.
- Teacher preview mode.
- Student progress monitor.
- Rejoin support.
- Duplicate name handling.
- Inappropriate nickname handling.
- Teacher override.

## Edge Cases

| Edge Case | Product Handling |
|---|---|
| Student disconnects | Student rejoins with same code/name/session token when possible. Work autosaves. |
| Student joins late | Teacher sees late join. Student enters current phase or teacher assigns catch-up access. |
| Student submits blank response | Submission blocked. Teacher sees incomplete status. |
| Student writes inappropriate content | System flags; teacher can remove from peer/reveal pool and reset student. |
| Student uses fake name | Teacher can rename or remove. Inappropriate name filter catches obvious cases. |
| Student tries to vote randomly | Justification required. Repeated low-effort votes flagged. |
| Teacher advances too early | System warns if many students are incomplete. Safe rollback allowed before peer assignments. |
| Teacher needs to pause for discussion | Pause freezes timers and shows paused state to students. |
| Class runs out of time | Teacher can skip extended reveal, use quick reflection, export unfinished round. |
| Device fails | Student can rejoin on another device using code and teacher-confirmed identity. |
| Wi-Fi drops | Autosave locally where possible and sync when connection returns. |
| Student needs accommodation | Teacher can extend time, allow partner mode, reset submission, or provide read-aloud support. |

## Off-Task Detection

MVP should avoid invasive surveillance. Reasonable signals:

- Student has not joined.
- Student has not submitted.
- Student has no annotations.
- Student submitted too quickly.
- Student peer justification is repeated or too short.

Do not track keystrokes or attempt creepy behavior monitoring.

## 24. Anti-Garbage Safeguards

Students will try to do lazy work. The product must expect it without becoming punitive by default.

| Safeguard | Behaviour It Prevents | How It Works | Risk of Over-Policing | Teacher Override |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Highlight limit | Highlighting entire passage. | Teacher sets max highlights or word percentage. | Some students need broad annotation early. | Teacher can raise limit. |
| Over-highlight warning | Random highlighting. | Warns when too much text is selected. | May annoy careful students. | Teacher can disable. |
| Required evidence selection | Unsupported claims. | Students must select text before submitting. | Some prompts may need broader evidence. | Teacher can allow multiple quotes later. |
| Quote length limit | Whole-paragraph dumping. | Selected evidence has max length. | Poetry or speeches may need longer chunks. | Teacher can adjust. |
| Claim minimum | One-word claims. | Blocks claims below minimum. | Short valid claims rare but possible. | Teacher reset/approve. |
| Reasoning minimum | Evidence with no explanation. | Blocks missing reasoning. | Weak writers may struggle. | Sentence starters and accommodation. |
| Quote-repeat warning | Explanations that repeat evidence. | Detects heavy overlap between quote and reasoning. | False positives possible. | Teacher can ignore. |
| Peer justification required | Peer votes with no reasoning. | Vote cannot submit without explanation. | Slows round. | Teacher can shorten requirement in compressed mode. |
| Anonymous peer review | Friend voting. | Names hidden and pairings randomized. | Students may recognize writing style. | Teacher can remove identifying responses. |
| Self-review block | Gaming peer review. | Student’s own response excluded. | None. | No override needed. |
| Repeated justification flag | Copy-paste peer feedback. | Flags identical or near-identical justifications. | Common sentence starters may trigger. | Teacher reviews flag. |
| Inappropriate name filter | Bad nicknames. | Blocks obvious profanity/slurs. | False positives. | Teacher can approve/rename. |
| Inappropriate content flag | Unsafe submissions. | Flags profanity/slurs for teacher review. | Context matters in texts. | Teacher decides. |
| Submit-too-fast warning | Submitting without reading. | Warns if submission happens before reasonable reading time. | Strong readers may be fast. | Teacher can ignore. |
| Spam click throttling | Clicking everything. | Rate-limits repeated actions. | Rare accessibility issue. | Teacher can reset. |
| Nonsense flag | Gibberish to unlock next phase. | Flags extremely low-quality random text. | ELL or struggling writing may be misread. | Teacher judgment required. |
| AI-response caution | AI-generated student responses. | Flag polished, generic, off-prompt responses only as suspicious, not proof. | False accusations. | Teacher handles privately. |

The goal is redirection, not punishment.

## 28. UI / UX Design Principles

## Design Direction

Receipts should feel:

- Smart.
- Clean.
- Focused.
- Slightly competitive.
- Classroom-safe.
- Mature enough for junior high.
- Not babyish.
- Not corporate.
- Not cluttered.
- Not overly gamey.

## Visual Hierarchy

Priority order on student screens:

1. Current task.
2. Passage/prompt.
3. Required action.
4. Timer/status.
5. Support tools.
6. Game feedback.

Do not let points, badges, or decorative graphics compete with reading.

## Typography Guidance

- Use a highly readable sans-serif for interface.
- Use comfortable line height for passages.
- Allow font size adjustment.
- Keep passage width readable.
- Avoid tiny rubric text.
- Avoid decorative fonts.

## Colour Usage

- Use colour to show phase, tags, status, and warnings.
- Do not rely on colour alone.
- Annotation tags need icons or labels.
- Use high contrast.
- Avoid childish neon palettes.
- Use competition accents sparingly.

## Button Hierarchy

| Button Type | Usage |
|---|---|
| Primary | Main action: submit, continue, revise. |
| Secondary | Edit, back, view prompt. |
| Danger | Remove response, end round, delete passage. |
| Disabled | Locked phase or incomplete required fields. |

## Timer Design

Timer should be visible but not stressful.

- Teacher controls timers.
- Students see current phase timer.
- Timer colour shifts only near the end.
- Paused state must be obvious.
- No panic animations.

## Teacher Projection Mode

Projection mode must be:

- Large.
- Clean.
- Anonymous by default.
- Minimal controls.
- Readable from the back of the room.

## Student Device Considerations

Students may use:

- Chromebooks.
- School laptops.
- Tablets.
- Phones if allowed.

Design must work best on laptop/Chromebook and remain usable on tablets. Phone support is secondary but should not break.

## Avoiding Cognitive Overload

- One major task per screen.
- Short instructions.
- Visible success criteria.
- No side quests.
- No stores.
- No avatars.
- No unnecessary menus during a live round.

## Make Reading the Center

The passage should be the largest and clearest element during reading, annotation, and evidence selection. Everything else supports the text.

## UI Mistakes to Avoid

- Crowding the passage with game graphics.
- Hiding the prompt.
- Letting students lose selected evidence.
- Tiny annotation controls.
- Too many tags at once.
- Public shame leaderboards.
- Confusing teacher and student controls.
- Making setup feel like building an LMS course.
- Treating scoring as more important than revision.

## States

| State | Who Can Enter It | Who Can Leave It | Teacher Controls Available | Student Actions Allowed | System Events | Failure Cases |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Draft | Teacher creates setup. | Teacher launches. | Edit passage, prompt, settings. | None. | Save draft. | Missing required fields. |
| Lobby | Teacher launches round. | Teacher starts reading. | Start, lock, remove/rename students. | Join, edit name if allowed. | Join code active. | Fake names, duplicate names. |
| Reading | Teacher starts. | Teacher advances. | Pause, extend, shorten, advance. | Read passage. | Timer runs, autosave position optional. | Student skims, joins late. |
| Annotating | Teacher advances. | Teacher advances. | Pause, timer, monitor heatmap. | Highlight and tag. | Save annotations. | Over-highlighting. |
| Prompt | Teacher reveals prompt. | Teacher advances. | Clarify, pause, advance. | Read prompt. | Prompt displayed. | Prompt confusion. |
| Submitting | Teacher opens submission. | Teacher locks/advances. | Lock/unlock, timer, monitor status. | Select evidence, claim, reason, submit. | Validate fields, autosave. | Blank/nonsense submissions. |
| PeerReview | Teacher triggers. | Teacher advances. | Remove response, monitor justifications. | Compare anonymous responses, justify vote. | Assign pairs, save votes. | Too few submissions, self-review bug. |
| Revising | Teacher opens. | Teacher advances. | Timer, spotlight examples, monitor revisions. | Revise and explain changes. | Save revision. | No meaningful revision. |
| Reveal | Teacher triggers. | Teacher advances. | Choose trends/examples, hide/reveal names. | View results, discuss. | Generate trends. | Sensitive info exposed. |
| Reflection | Teacher opens. | Teacher completes. | Timer, monitor completion. | Submit reflection. | Save reflection. | Generic responses. |
| Complete | Teacher ends. | None except view/export. | Export, review dashboard. | View completion message. | Lock round. | Export issue. |
| Paused | Teacher pauses from active state. | Teacher resumes. | Resume, end round. | View paused message. | Freeze timers. | Students think work lost. |
| Cancelled | Teacher cancels. | None. | Export partial if available. | Round ended message. | Lock all actions. | Accidental cancellation. |

## Practical Implementation Rules

- `Paused` should preserve previous active state.
- Phase changes must be server-authoritative.
- Student clients subscribe to round state.
- Student actions must be validated against current phase.
- Peer review assignments should generate only after submissions are locked or teacher confirms.
- Reveal should never show names unless teacher explicitly enables names.
- Round completion should lock all student edits.
- Autosave should run for annotations, submissions, revisions, and reflections.

## 36. Example Gameplay Scenario

## Grade Level

Grade 8 ELA

## Passage Type

Short story excerpt

## Passage Summary

Students read a two-page excerpt about a student named Lena who refuses to tell her friend that she lost an important class project. Instead, Lena avoids eye contact, changes the subject, and becomes defensive when questioned.

## Challenge Prompt

**What best explains Lena’s behavior: anger, guilt, or fear? Choose the strongest evidence and explain how it supports your claim.**

## Teacher Setup

Teacher selects:

- Passage: “The Missing Project”
- Focus skill: Character motivation
- Tags: Important Evidence, Character, Inference, Conflict, Confusion
- Reading timer: 6 minutes
- Annotation timer: 5 minutes
- Submission timer: 8 minutes
- Peer review: anonymous side-by-side
- Revision timer: 6 minutes

## Student Reading Task

Students read the passage and look for clues about why Lena behaves the way she does.

## Annotation Examples

### Strong Annotation

Text:

> “Lena stared at the corner of her desk and folded the torn rubric until it was too small to fold again.”

Tag:

**Inference**

Why strong:

This suggests guilt or anxiety without directly saying it.

### Weak Annotation

Text:

> “Lena walked into class.”

Tag:

**Character**

Why weak:

It names an action but does not reveal motivation.

## Strong Evidence Example

> “When Marcus asked about the project, Lena stared at the corner of her desk and folded the torn rubric until it was too small to fold again.”

Why strong:

It connects directly to the moment she is questioned and shows avoidance, nervous behavior, and the damaged rubric.

## Weak Evidence Example

> “Lena walked into class late.”

Why weak:

It may be related to the situation, but it does not prove why she acts defensive.

## Strong Claim Example

> Lena is acting out of guilt because she knows she damaged the project and is trying to avoid admitting it.

## Weak Claim Example

> Lena is mad.

Why weak:

Too vague. It does not explain what caused the emotion or connect to the evidence.

## Strong Reasoning Example

> The torn rubric matters because it suggests Lena has already damaged or mishandled the project. Her refusal to look at Marcus when he asks about it shows she is avoiding the truth. This supports guilt more than anger because she is hiding responsibility, not attacking him first.

## Weak Reasoning Example

> This proves she is guilty because she folded the paper and looked down.

Why weak:

It repeats the evidence but does not explain why those actions show guilt.

## Peer Comparison Example

Response A:

> Lena is guilty because she looks down when Marcus asks about the project. She also folds the torn rubric over and over. This shows she knows something went wrong and does not want to admit it.

Response B:

> Lena is angry because Marcus asks her about the project and she does not answer him. This proves she is angry because she is not talking.

Student chooses:

**Response A**

## Peer Justification Example

> Response A is stronger because it uses two details together: the torn rubric and Lena avoiding eye contact. It explains why those details show guilt. Response B only says she is not talking, but silence could mean different things.

## Revision Example

Original:

> Lena is scared because she looks down. This shows she is scared because she looks down.

Revised:

> Lena is guilty because she avoids Marcus right after he asks about the project, and the torn rubric suggests she may have damaged it. Looking down matters because she has a reason to hide what happened.

Change explanation:

> I changed scared to guilty because the torn rubric is better evidence. My first answer repeated the quote, but my revision explains why the detail matters.

## Teacher Dashboard Insight

Dashboard shows:

- 55% chose guilt.
- 25% chose fear.
- 15% chose anger.
- Most selected evidence: torn rubric and avoiding eye contact.
- Common reasoning gap: students repeated the action without explaining motivation.
- Strong response cluster: students who connected damaged object + avoidance.
- Weak response cluster: students who used “walked in late” as evidence.

## Teacher-Led Discussion Follow-Up

Teacher asks:

1. “Why is the torn rubric stronger evidence than walking in late?”
2. “Can fear and guilt both be defensible? What would each need as evidence?”
3. “What makes Response A’s reasoning stronger than just repeating the quote?”
4. “Who changed their claim during revision? What made the revision stronger?”

## Student Reflection Example

> I learned that evidence has to prove the exact claim. My first quote only showed Lena was nervous, but my revised evidence showed why guilt made more sense.
